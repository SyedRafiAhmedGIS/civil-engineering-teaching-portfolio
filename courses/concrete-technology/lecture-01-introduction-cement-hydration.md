# Lecture 1 Outline — Introduction to Concrete and Cement Hydration

**Duration:** 50 minutes · **Format:** lecture with two think-pair-share breaks ·
**Course:** Concrete Technology · **Week:** 2 (cement chemistry block)

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture students will be able to: (1) name the four main clinker phases
and state the role of each; (2) write the overall hydration reaction of C3S and identify
C-S-H and portlandite as products; (3) explain why concrete cures *continues* to gain
strength over weeks; and (4) select a suitable cement type for a given exposure.

## Slide deck (titles and key points)

1. **Why concrete?**
   - Most-used man-made material; local raw materials; formable; fire resistant
   - Weaknesses: tension, brittleness, cracks -> motivates the whole course
2. **What is Portland cement?**
   - Limestone + clay, clinker burned ~1450 C, gypsum added for set control
3. **The four clinker phases**
   - C3S: early strength; C2S: later strength; C3A: flash set, heat; C4AF: flux, colour
   - Think-pair-share: why does rapid-set cement have high C3S and C3A?
4. **Hydration chemistry**
   - 2C3S + 6H -> C-S-H + 3CH; C-S-H glue model; ~23% water chemically bound
5. **From paste to concrete**
   - Paste binds sand and coarse aggregate; ITZ concept preview
6. **Heat evolution curve**
   - Five stages; dormant period is why we have time to transport and place
7. **Cement types and exposure selection**
   - Type I / II / III / IV / V logic; sulfate exposure example
8. **Closing clicker question**
   - A 28-day strength problem: why would a Type III cube beat Type I at 7 days but tie at 90?

## In-class activities

- Two think-pair-share moments (phases and cement selection), 4 minutes each.
- Board sketch: students draw the heat evolution curve from memory at minute 40.

## Assessment hook

Quiz 2 (week 4) re-uses the closing clicker question format; the cube-strength lab later
connects early-strength differences to real 7-day vs 28-day data.

## Common misconceptions to address

- "Cement dries" - it hydrates; water is a reactant, not just a lubricant.
- "More cement is always stronger" - W/C ratio dominates; over-cemented mixes crack.
